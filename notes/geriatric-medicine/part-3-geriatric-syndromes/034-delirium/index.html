Delirium

Acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness in older adults — pathophysiology, screening tools, evaluation, and a multicomponent management framework.

Updated 19 July 2026

Part 3: Geriatric Syndromes

Overview & Definition

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness accompanied by additional cognitive change, arising over hours to days as a direct physiological consequence of an underlying medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, or toxin exposure. It is best understood not as a discrete neuropsychiatric diagnosis but as the final common pathway by which a vulnerable brain signals systemic decompensation — a form of acute, usually reversible brain failure analogous to acute kidney injury or acute respiratory failure in other organ systems.

Delirium is the most common serious geriatric syndrome encountered across care settings, yet it remains under-recognized in up to 60–70% of cases, largely because the hypoactive subtype is mistaken for fatigue, depression, or “just being an older patient.” Every episode of delirium should be treated as a marker of potentially serious underlying illness until proven otherwise, and its detection should trigger a structured search for a precipitant rather than passive observation.

Epidemiology in Older Adults

Delirium incidence and prevalence rise steeply with age, baseline cognitive impairment, and severity of acute illness:

SettingApproximate incidence/prevalence
General medical inpatient wards15–30%
Elective major surgery10–20%
High-risk surgery (hip fracture repair, cardiac surgery)up to 50%
Intensive care unit (especially mechanically ventilated patients)50–80%
Emergency department presentations8–17%, frequently unrecognized

Delirium is an independent marker of poor outcome, not merely a bystander finding: postoperative delirium is associated with a 7–10% absolute increase in 30-day mortality and 2–3 additional hospital days; delirium at emergency department presentation carries roughly a 70% increase in 6-month mortality; and ICU delirium is associated with a 2- to 4-fold increase in overall mortality. The estimated aggregate healthcare cost attributable to delirium in the United States exceeds $150 billion annually, driven by prolonged hospitalization, increased institutionalization, and long-term care costs following an episode.

Age-Related Pathophysiology

Contemporary models frame delirium as an interaction between predisposing vulnerability and precipitating insult — a patient with high baseline vulnerability can develop delirium from a comparatively minor stressor, while a patient with low vulnerability requires a major physiological insult to decompensate.

Predisposing (vulnerability) factors, which reduce cognitive reserve:

  • Advanced age and pre-existing dementia or mild cognitive impairment (present in up to two-thirds of hospitalized patients with delirium, often unrecognized beforehand)
  • Frailty, multimorbidity, and functional dependence
  • Sensory impairment (uncorrected vision or hearing loss)
  • Malnutrition and dehydration
  • Prior episode of delirium
  • Substance use disorder

Precipitating (insult) factors, which trigger decompensation:

  • Infection (urinary tract infection and pneumonia are classically under-recognized triggers in older adults)
  • Surgery, anesthesia, and critical illness
  • Metabolic derangement (hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, hypo-/hyperglycemia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy)
  • Medications — particularly anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids (at both excessive and inadequately treated pain doses), and any recent medication change; drug effects account for up to 39% of cases
  • Untreated pain, constipation, or urinary retention
  • Sleep deprivation, immobilization, and sensory deprivation (missing glasses or hearing aids)
  • Unfamiliar or overstimulating environments, including the ICU

Age-related reductions in cerebral perfusion, synaptic density, and neurotransmitter reserve lower the threshold at which these insults produce clinical decompensation — this is why a urinary tract infection or a bout of constipation can precipitate florid delirium in a frail 85-year-old with underlying dementia, while the same insult produces no cognitive change in a healthy young adult. At the mechanistic level, several overlapping processes have been implicated: neuroinflammation with blood–brain barrier disruption, cholinergic deficiency relative to dopaminergic excess, oxidative stress, a dysregulated neuroendocrine (cortisol) stress response, circadian and melatonin disruption, and — increasingly supported by neuroimaging and electrophysiology — impaired connectivity within frontoparietal and thalamocortical attention networks. No single mechanism is sufficient on its own; delirium is best conceptualized as a failure of coordinated large-scale brain network function under physiological stress rather than an isolated neurotransmitter abnormality.

Clinical Features

SubtypePresentationRecognition
HyperactiveAgitation, restlessness, hallucinations, combative or uncooperative behaviorUsually detected promptly
HypoactiveSomnolence, withdrawal, reduced psychomotor activity, quiet confusionMost common in older adults; frequently missed and mistaken for fatigue or depression; carries higher morbidity and mortality
MixedFluctuation between hyperactive and hypoactive states over the course of a dayDetection depends on timing of assessment

Core clinical features, regardless of subtype, are an acute onset (hours to days, representing a clear change from baseline) and a fluctuating course, often worse in the evening (“sundowning”). Inattention is the cardinal finding — patients struggle to follow a conversation, are easily distracted, or cannot maintain a coherent train of thought. Additional features include disorganized thinking, altered level of consciousness (from hypervigilance to stupor), perceptual disturbances such as illusions or hallucinations, sleep–wake cycle reversal, and emotional lability.

Because families and caregivers spend the most time with the patient, their observations are often the first clue to a hypoactive presentation — comments such as “they’re sleeping much more than usual,” “they’ve barely eaten in days,” or “I thought they were just depressed” should prompt formal delirium screening rather than reassurance.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Delirium is a clinical diagnosis; no laboratory biomarker has been validated for diagnosis or monitoring. Diagnostic criteria require all of the following:

  1. Acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness
  2. At least one additional cognitive disturbance (memory, orientation, language, visuospatial ability, or perception)
  3. Not better explained by a pre-existing, established, or evolving neurocognitive disorder
  4. Not occurring in the context of a severely reduced level of arousal or coma
  5. Evidence that the disturbance is a direct physiological consequence of a medical condition, intoxication, withdrawal, or toxin exposure

Bedside screening tools:

  • Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) — the most widely used and validated tool (sensitivity 94–100%, specificity 90–95%). Diagnosis requires acute onset/fluctuating course and inattention, plus either disorganized thinking or altered level of consciousness. Adapted variants include the CAM-ICU (nonverbal tasks for ventilated patients) and versions for the emergency department and long-term care.
  • 4AT — a brief (under 2 minutes), no-training-required tool with sensitivity ~76% and specificity ~94%, well suited to busy acute-care settings.

History should always include a collateral informant: baseline cognitive and functional status, timeline of the change, a careful medication reconciliation (new drugs, dose changes, cumulative anticholinergic burden), review for infectious or metabolic triggers, recent falls, and any change in environment or caregiving arrangements.

Physical examination should be complete (cardiac, pulmonary, neurologic, abdominal, musculoskeletal, and skin) with attention to vital signs, hydration status, and signs of occult infection.

Initial workup, guided by history and examination rather than reflexively ordered in full, typically includes complete blood count, basic/complete metabolic panel, urinalysis, chest radiography, electrocardiography, and bladder scan for retention; arterial blood gas and blood cultures are added when clinically indicated. Brain imaging is not routine — it should be reserved for new focal neurologic signs, recent trauma, unexplained depressed consciousness, or suspected intracranial infection or hemorrhage, since focal deficits (not anticoagulation status alone) are the strongest predictor of an abnormal scan.

Differential Diagnosis

  • Dementia — chronic and stable rather than acute and fluctuating, though delirium is frequently superimposed on underlying dementia and can be difficult to separate without collateral history
  • Depression — can mimic hypoactive delirium; look for acuity and fluctuation, which favor delirium
  • Primary psychotic disorders
  • Nonconvulsive status epilepticus
  • Catatonia
  • Structural CNS lesions (stroke, mass, hemorrhage)
  • Sensory-deprivation-related confusion in patients without glasses or hearing aids

Management

Treat the precipitant first. The single highest-yield intervention is identifying and reversing the underlying cause(s) — treating infection, correcting metabolic derangements, discontinuing or replacing offending medications, relieving urinary retention or constipation, and controlling pain.

Nonpharmacologic, multicomponent care is the primary treatment, since no medication is approved for prevention or treatment of delirium itself. The Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) model, endorsed by the American Geriatrics Society, bundles:

  • Reorientation aids (clocks, calendars, frequent reorientation) and cognitive stimulation
  • Sensory optimization — ensuring glasses and hearing aids are worn during the day
  • Sleep protocols that minimize nighttime disruption (reduced overnight vitals/medication timing, noise and light reduction) rather than sedative use
  • Early and frequent mobilization, with removal of unnecessary tethers (urinary catheters, IV lines) that limit movement
  • Adequate hydration and nutrition
  • Family and caregiver presence and involvement

This approach reduces incident delirium by roughly a third to a half in at-risk populations, lowers falls by about 40%, and is cost-saving compared with usual care.

Medication review and deprescribing should systematically remove or minimize anticholinergics, benzodiazepines (outside of withdrawal states), and other high-risk agents, cross-referenced against the Beers Criteria and cumulative anticholinergic burden.

Pharmacologic therapy has a narrow, specific role and is never first-line for prevention or routine treatment:

  • Substance-withdrawal delirium (e.g., benzodiazepines for alcohol withdrawal)
  • Severe agitation in hyperactive delirium that poses a safety risk to the patient or others — low-dose antipsychotics (haloperidol, quetiapine, risperidone) used briefly, at the lowest effective dose, with ECG monitoring for QTc prolongation; quetiapine is preferred and haloperidol avoided in Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia
  • Symptom control in delirium at the end of life

Contemporary evidence does not support antipsychotics to shorten delirium duration, hasten cognitive recovery, or prevent new episodes — their use should be reserved strictly for safety-threatening agitation. Melatonin, cholinesterase inhibitors, and off-label agents (valproate, gabapentinoids, nonbenzodiazepine sedatives) lack demonstrated benefit and may worsen sedation or mobility.

Effective management depends on an interprofessional team — nursing (often first to detect subtle change), pharmacy (medication reconciliation and anticholinergic burden review), physical and occupational therapy (mobilization), and the primary or geriatric medicine team coordinating the overall plan with the patient’s family.

Complications, Prognosis & Special Considerations

Complications include functional decline, falls and related injury, aspiration pneumonia, pressure injuries, malnutrition and dehydration, prolonged hospitalization, new institutionalization, and — increasingly recognized — persistent long-term cognitive impairment beyond the acute episode.

Prognosis is guarded. Delirium can persist well beyond hospital discharge (present in roughly a third of patients at one month), and accumulating longitudinal evidence shows that an episode of delirium independently accelerates cognitive decline and increases the risk of incident dementia, even after apparent clinical resolution — delirium is not merely a transient confusional state but a marker of, and possible contributor to, lasting brain injury.

Special considerations in frailty and multimorbidity: frail patients and those with pre-existing cognitive impairment have a markedly lower threshold for delirium from insults that would not affect a robust older adult, so a disproportionately vigorous response to a seemingly minor change in a frail patient should raise suspicion for delirium rather than be dismissed. Polypharmacy compounds risk through cumulative anticholinergic and sedative burden, making medication review a recurring rather than one-time task. Delirium superimposed on dementia is common, harder to detect against a background of chronic cognitive impairment, and carries worse outcomes than delirium alone — collateral history establishing the patient’s true baseline is essential. In refractory delirium at the end of life, goals-of-care discussions should guide the balance between symptom control and further diagnostic evaluation.

Key Points

  • Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention with an underlying medical cause — treat it as a marker of serious illness, not a standalone diagnosis.
  • Hypoactive delirium is the most common presentation in older adults and the most frequently missed; a change reported by caregivers deserves formal screening (CAM or 4AT).
  • The evaluation should be targeted, not exhaustive — history, examination, and first-line labs identify most precipitants; brain imaging is reserved for focal or red-flag findings.
  • Treating the underlying precipitant and multicomponent nonpharmacologic care (the HELP model) are the primary interventions — there is no approved medication for prevention or treatment.
  • Reserve antipsychotics for safety-threatening agitation only, at the lowest effective dose and briefest duration, with ECG monitoring.
  • An episode of delirium is associated with long-term cognitive decline, not just acute morbidity — recovery should be followed, not assumed.
