Journal: Journal of Indian Academy of Geriatrics 2026;22:147-52

Authors: Purohit PP, Bhasker S, Bhatia SK, Aggarwal V, Karade S, Sethi D

DOI: 10.4103/jiag.jiag_8_26

Frailty through the Lens of Chronic Low-grade Inflammation in Older Adults: A Cross-sectional Study

Cross-sectional study of 387 older adults attending a tertiary care OPD, examining the prevalence of frailty and prefrailty by Fried's criteria and their association with inflammatory markers (hsCRP, IL-6) and geriatric syndromes.

Published 18 June 2026
AgingFrailtyInflammagingInflammatory Markers

Abstract

Background: Frailty is recognized as a geriatric giant that is associated with vulnerability and poor health outcomes in older adults. The interrelationship between frailty and systemic inflammation (inflammaging) is poorly studied, especially in the population residing in developing countries. The objective of the study was to study the prevalence of frailty and prefrailty and its associations with inflammatory markers and geriatric syndromes.

Methodology: A cross-sectional observational study was conducted in 387 older adults aged ≥60 years attending outpatient departments (OPDs) in a tertiary care hospital. Frailty assessment was done by Fried’s criteria. Inflammatory markers included interleukin-6 (IL-6) and highly sensitive C-reactive protein (hsCRP). Inflammatory markers were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay-based assays. Statistical analysis employed the Chi-square test, Kruskal–Wallis test, and logistic regression analysis.

Results: Frailty was present in 19.9% of subjects, with prefrailty in 37%, while 43.1% of subjects were nonfrail. There were significant associations between frailty and decreased calf circumference (P < 0.001), smoking (P < 0.001), hypertension (P = 0.010), diabetes mellitus (P = 0.042), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (P = 0.047), falls (P < 0.001), urinary incontinence (P = 0.019), and depression (P < 0.001). hsCRP and IL-6 levels showed significant elevation with increasing frailty severity (P < 0.001 and P = 0.005, respectively). Logistic regression identified calf circumference, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, falls, incontinence, and depression as independent predictors of frailty.

Conclusion: Frailty and prefrailty were prevalent among older adults attending OPD in a tertiary care hospital. Indian older adults have a significant association of frailty with inflammatory markers. Early identification and targeted intervention addressing reversible risk factors may help in improving the outcomes in older adults.

Keywords: Aging, frailty, inflammaging, inflammatory markers

Introduction/Background

Frailty is an aging-related geriatric syndrome characterized by increased vulnerability and poor health outcomes to stressors due to a decline in physiological reserves and function across multiple systems and domains.[1-3] Identifying these frail older adults at an earlier stage is of utmost importance as timely interventions will enable them to cope with acute stressors more effectively, thus decreasing the morbidity and mortality.

Frailty and aging immune system (immunesenescence) is characterized by a chronic, continuous low-grade systemic inflammatory state, which is called inflammaging.[4] Inflammatory markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines like highly sensitive C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and interleukin (IL-6) have shown an increasing trend with aging, especially in older adults with frailty.[5] Increase in IL-6 in the geriatric population has been noted to be associated with multiple pathophysiological processes, including disability, atherosclerosis, osteosarcopenia, functional decline, and all-cause morbidity and mortality.[6-8]

Data on frailty and inflammaging from developing countries like India are very limited. Hence, this study was planned to estimate the prevalence of frailty in older adults (>60 years) attending the outpatient department (OPD) and its association with inflammatory markers like hsCRP and IL-6. The primary objective of the study was to estimate the prevalence of frailty and prefrailty using Fried’s Frailty Index and its association with various other factors like age, gender, comorbidities, anthropometric indices (calf circumference, midarm circumference, weight, and body mass index [BMI]), and other geriatric syndromes. The secondary objective was to investigate the distribution of IL-6 and hsCRP levels in the sample population and examine their association with frailty, using the above inflammatory markers.

Methodology

An observational cross-sectional study was conducted in geriatric patients visiting the OPD. Subjects were enrolled from the medical and geriatric OPD of a tertiary care hospital in Western India from June 2018 to June 2021. The inclusion criteria were age of more than 60 years of age and independent for activities of daily living. Patients with chronic kidney disease, Child’s C cirrhosis, thyroid disorders, hospitalization in the last 3 months, and history of femoral fracture/fragility fracture were excluded from the study.

Baseline anthropometric data (height, weight, BMI, midupper arm circumference, and calf circumference) were measured for all the patients. Assessment of frailty was done using Fried’s Frailty Index. Estimation of gait speed was done by the time taken to walk 4.6 m on level ground. A gait speed of more than 7.2 s was classified as a slow gait speed. Hand grip strength measurement was done using a digital hand grip dynamometer. Weak hand grip was defined as per the available normograms already fed into the device after adjusting for age and gender. Detailed systemic examination was carried out for all the patients.

Based on the frailty assessment, the subjects were grouped into three groups, namely:

  • Robust older adults (frailty score 0)
  • Prefrail (frailty score 1–2)
  • Frail elderly (frailty score ≥3)

All the patients underwent blood tests for inflammatory markers (hsCRP and IL-6) with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay-based kits. Other blood investigations included complete blood count, creatinine, serum albumin, blood sugar, and liver function tests.

The association between frailty scores was studied with hsCRP, IL-6, anthropometric parameters, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors (physical activity and addictions). All the patients with frailty and prefrailty were educated about the disease and were managed with a high-protein diet, regular exercise schedule, along with weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening exercises. All the other associated comorbidities were identified and treated as per standard guidelines in all the patients.

A sample size was taken, as 384, assuming a prevalence of frailty being 50% in the geriatric population in a developing country like India. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

The data were integrated into the Excel sheet and were analyzed by the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software package (version 25), IBM Corporation (headquartered in Armonk, New York, USA). The prevalence of frailty was estimated using descriptive statistics. The association of frailty with different anthropometric variables (weight, BMI, calf circumference, and midupper arm circumference) and morbidities was done by Chi-square test. The association of inflammatory markers (hsCRP and IL-6) with frailty was estimated using Kruskal–Wallis test.

Results

After initial screening, 387 patients were enrolled in the study, of whom around two-thirds (65.4%) of the subjects were male and one-third (34.6%) were female. Patients were enrolled from the medical and geriatric OPD. The mean age of the study population was 67.81 years, out of which 26.4% (102/387) were above 70 years of age.

It was noted that 7.2% (28/387) of patients had poor nutrition with a BMI of <18.5 kg/m², and 7.8% (30/387) were overweight/obese with a BMI of more than 25 kg/m². Around one fourth (26.9%) of patients had reduced calf circumference of <33.5 cm. It was noted that 48.6% (188/387) of the subjects were vegetarian and 51.4% (199/387) were nonvegetarian. It was seen that 27.1% (105/387) had a history of smoking, 17.1% (66/387) consumed alcohol, and 16.3% (66/387) had a history of chewing tobacco.

The most common comorbidity in our study subjects was hypertension, seen in 52.5% (203/387) of subjects, followed by diabetes mellitus (41.3%), benign prostatic hypertrophy (31.8%), falls (18.3%), and coronary artery disease (17.6%). Unintentional weight loss was seen in 14.2% of the study subjects. Other geriatric syndromes (falls and urinary incontinence) were seen in 31% of patients. The details are shown in Table 1.

In frailty indices, almost 50% of the elderly were not aware of their previous weight, so exact weight loss could not be documented. The minimum weight was 34 kg, and the maximum weight was 110 kg. Twenty-four patients had a weight of <45 kg, and 11 patients had a weight of more than 80 kg. Low physical activity was seen in 15.2% of the study subjects, whereas self-reported exhaustion was seen in 22.73% of the study subjects. Reduced hand grip strength was noted in 41.8% of the study subjects. The minimum hand grip strength was 4.1 kg, whereas the maximum hand grip strength was 53.23 kg. The mean hand grip strength in the study population was 24.59 kg. It was seen that 26.6% of the patients in our study had a slow gait speed as estimated by the time taken of more than 7.2 s to walk 4.6 m on level ground at one’s own pace.

It was noted that 19.9% (77/387) of the study population was frail with Fried’s frailty score of ≥3. Prefrailty, with a Fried’s frailty score of either 1 or 2, was seen in 37% (143/387) of the study subjects. It was seen that only 43.1% (167/387) of the older adult population attending OPD were classified as nonfrail (robust) with a Fried’s frailty score of 0.

There was a direct correlation between the age and frailty scores of the older adults (P = 0.006). Decreased calf circumference <33.5 cm was also associated with frailty (P = 0.001). Smoking was also associated with an increased risk of frailty (P < 0.001). There was no association of frailty scores with gender (P = 0.127), BMI (P = 0.058), alcohol consumption (P = 0.152), and tobacco chewing (P = 0.504).

Frailty and prefrailty were seen to be associated with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and benign prostatic enlargement, with P values of 0.010, 0.042, 0.047, and < 0.001, respectively. Among geriatric syndromes, frailty was significantly associated with urinary incontinence (P = 0.019) and falls (P < 0.001). In geriatric mental health disorders, depression was seen to be significantly associated with frailty and prefrailty (P < 0.001). However, there was no association noted between frailty and prefrailty with coronary artery disease (P = 0.635) and cognitive decline (P = 0.155). The details are shown in Table 2.

Multiple comparisons were done using Bonferroni correction, and it was seen that there was a significant association of frailty score with calf circumference (P < 0.001), smoking (P < 0.001), hypertension (P = 0.011), diabetes (P = 0.022), COPD (P < 0.001), falls (P < 0.001), and depression (P < 0.001).

Discussion

In our study, frailty was present in 19.9% of the older adults attending OPD as assessed by Fried’s Frailty Index, whereas 37% were prefrail and only 43.1% were robust. Similar findings were noted in a study from Germany, where frailty as per Fried’s phenotype was noted in 17.8% and prefrailty was seen in 43.4%.[9] In a recent meta-analysis of 56 studies, it was seen that although the prevalence of frailty varies in different studies and different geographical regions, the pooled prevalence of frailty was 17.4%, and the prevalence of prefrailty was 49.3%.[10] These findings were in agreement with the above study.

In an Indian study from West Bengal, the prevalence of frailty was much more and 38.8% elderly above 60 years of age were found to be frail, with age, female sex, economic dependency, more than two chronic comorbidities, and illiteracy being associated with frailty.[11] In another Malaysian study, the prevalence of frailty was 18.3%. In this study, it was noted that age, lower BMI, self-reported exhaustion, and admission to a hospital in the previous 1 year were positively associated with frailty.[12] The findings in the above studies are in accordance with our findings, despite our being a hospital-based study.

In an OPD-based study done in a geriatric clinic using Fried’s Frailty Index and Gill’s Frailty Index, it was noted that around 40% of the OPD patients were frail with either of the assessment tools, and 29% were frail with both the tools.[13] In another study done at a frailty clinic, it was seen that in patients of cardioembolic disease, the prevalence of frailty varied between 24% and 41%.[14] Our study was OPD based study done in patients who were independent for activities of daily living. In our study, we may have underestimated the prevalence of frailty as we excluded patients with impaired activities of daily living and with chronic systemic morbidities who are likely to be frail.

We studied different frailty indices as per Fried’s Phenotypic Frailty Index, which included decreased hand grip strength, decreased gait speed, self-reported exhaustion, low activity index, and weight loss, which was unintentional. In addition, other anthropometric markers of frailty, such as calf circumference, midupper arm circumference, and BMI, were also measured. In our study, the most common frailty indicator, as per Fried’s Frailty Index, was reduced hand grip strength (50.4%), followed by slow gait speed (29.6%), self-reported exhaustion (22.73%), low physical activity (15.2%), and weight loss (14.2%). This is in contradiction to a Western study where the prevalence of frailty indicators as per Fried’s frailty criteria was much less, with the most common factor being low exercise capacity (19.6%), followed by reduced hand grip strength (15.4%), slow gait speed (10.4%), self-reported exhaustion (7.3%), and weight loss (2.1%). It was also noted in the above study that sensitivity for frailty was maximum for hand grip strength and low activity levels whereas specificity was maximum for weight loss and decreased gait speed.[15]

The Indian population has been noted to have decreased hand grip strength and pinch strength as compared to western population.[16] Even gait speed shows a significant difference with ethnicity, age, and gender.[17] Higher prevalence of frailty indicators in our study may be due to ethnic variation in the Indian population and the nonavailability of normograms of gait speed and hand grip strength specific to the Indian population.

In our study, the low calf circumference was also significantly associated with frailty, with P < 0.001. Calf circumference has a very good inverse relationship with frailty score, with low calf circumference showing a higher frailty score.[18] In a recent study, calf circumference was considered to be a novel tool for assessing the risk of disability in the older adult.[19] Calf circumference has also been seen to be associated with all-cause mortality, with a higher value predicting a lower all-cause mortality.[20] Similar findings were noted in another study done on frail elderly, where low calf circumference was seen to have a good predictive value for adverse health outcomes.[21] In another study, it was seen that gender specific cut off for calf circumference for screening for sarcopenia, with cut off value of 35 cm for men and 33 cm for women.[22] In our study, we took a cutoff of 33.5 cm for determining low calf circumference. Measurement of the calf circumference is a simple, reproducible, and cheap method for initial screening for frailty. Cut off for the specific population needs to be defined, and normograms are required to be developed.

Twenty-seven percent of the elderly gave a history of smoking, and it was seen that smoking was strongly associated with frailty, with P < 0.001. Similar findings were noted in a recent study, where it was seen that elderly patients with current smoking were much more likely to develop frailty over a 4-year follow-up period as compared to nonsmokers.[23] Similar findings were noted in a systematic review where smoking was found to be a predictor of worsening frailty in the elderly living in a community.[24] In our study, alcohol consumption was seen in 17.1% of the elderly population visiting OPDs and was not significantly associated with frailty, with P = 0.152. This may be due to the fact that we excluded the patients with advanced cirrhosis. In our study, logistic regression analysis showed that frailty was significantly associated with decreased calf circumference, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, falls, incontinence, and depression.

Frailty has been seen to be more common in females than in males.[10,25] In our study, gender did not have any significant association with frailty. This may be explained as males outnumbered females in our study, with two-thirds of the elderly being males and only one-third being females.

In our study, IL-6 and hsCRP were seen to be significantly associated with frailty and prefrailty, with P = 0.005 and <0.001, respectively. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, it was noted that frailty is an inflammatory disease of aging and has a strong association with an increase in the inflammatory markers like IL-6, CRP, total leukocyte count, and D-dimer. This elevation in the inflammatory markers was independent of the geographical region and population.[26] Similar findings were noted in another study where increased levels of IL-6 were positively associated with frail and prefrail elderly.[27] Similar findings of association between CRP and frailty have been seen in many other studies.[28-30]

Limitations

Limitations of our study were that it was an OPD based study and was done in patients who were independent for activities of daily living. A significant number of the elderly in our study were not aware of their baseline weight, which may have led to underassessment of frailty as per Fried’s Frailty Scale. Another limitation was that the hand grip strength and gait speed cutoffs used in our studies were based on the Western population, which may have led to overestimation of frailty in our study population. Another limitation was that severely frail patients who were not able to perform their activities of daily living were excluded from the study, and thus, this frailty estimation may not be a true estimate of frailty.

Conclusion

Frailty and prefrailty are important emerging geriatric syndromes in older adults, especially in developing countries like India. More than half of the elderly population who were independent for their ADLs were either frail or prefrail. Inflammation and frailty had a positive association, as seen by elevated hsCRP and IL-6 levels. Frailty was significantly associated with age, comorbidities, geriatric syndromes, and calf circumference. Screening and timely intervention of the geriatric population for frailty and prefrailty may be useful in preventing poor health outcomes and maintaining independent functioning in older adults.
