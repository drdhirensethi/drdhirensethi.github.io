Acute Delirium Unmasking Polypharmacy Harm: De-prescribing Using Beers Criteria

An 81-year-old retired officer with acute delirium, identified to have nine concurrent medications including multiple Beers Criteria culprits. A structured de-prescribing exercise guided by STOPP/START and Beers Criteria resolved the delirium and improved function.

Published 10 August 2025
DeliriumPolypharmacyBeers CriteriaSTOPP/STARTDe-prescribingCase Study

Key Learning Points

  • Acute delirium in older adults demands an immediate medication reconciliation — polypharmacy is the most common and most reversible precipitant.
  • The Beers Criteria 2023 update specifically flags anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, and first-generation antihistamines as high-risk in adults ≥65.
  • STOPP/START provides a structured framework for identifying drugs to stop (STOPP) and drugs that should be started but are missing (START).
  • De-prescribing is an active clinical skill, not a passive omission — it requires patient and family engagement, gradual tapering where indicated, and vigilant monitoring.
  • Functional improvement (Barthel Index improvement) and cognitive clarity (MoCA improvement) are the true endpoints of successful de-prescribing, not the medication count alone.

Case Presentation

An 81-year-old retired army officer was referred to the Geriatrics ward from the medical ward with a two-day history of acute confusion, agitation at night, and visual misperception. His family reported that he had been “not himself” since the previous week, with fluctuating attention and sleep-wake reversal.

Background: Hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (on insulin), benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), osteoarthritis of bilateral knees, chronic insomnia, and anxiety (managed by his general practitioner for the past four years).

Medications on admission (nine in total):

  1. Amlodipine 5 mg OD
  2. Telmisartan 40 mg OD
  3. Metformin 500 mg BD
  4. Insulin glargine 14 units at night
  5. Tamsulosin 0.4 mg OD
  6. Diclofenac SR 75 mg OD (for knee pain)
  7. Clonazepam 0.5 mg at night (for insomnia, prescribed 4 years ago)
  8. Hydroxyzine 25 mg BD (for anxiety)
  9. Chlorpheniramine 4 mg PRN (self-prescribed for itching)

Assessment

Delirium confirmation: CAM (Confusion Assessment Method) positive — acute onset, fluctuating course, inattention, and disorganised thinking.

CGA performed:

  • MoCA: 14/30 (baseline estimated at 24–25 from family account)
  • GDS-15: 7/15 (mild depression)
  • Barthel Index: 55/100 (moderate dependence — new from baseline of ~85)
  • Fried Frailty: Score 3 (Frail)

Beers Criteria review of the nine medications:

DrugBeers FlagConcern
Clonazepam 0.5 mgYES — strongBenzodiazepines: increased fall risk, delirium, cognitive impairment in adults ≥65
Hydroxyzine 25 mgYES — strongAnticholinergic: sedation, confusion, urinary retention; avoid in older adults
Chlorpheniramine 4 mgYES — strongFirst-generation antihistamine with high anticholinergic burden
Diclofenac SR 75 mgYESNSAIDs: GI bleeding risk, renal impairment, worsens hypertension and heart failure

STOPP criteria triggered: Benzodiazepine in older adult with fall risk; NSAID with renal function check pending; anticholinergic load (cumulative).

Management

Phase 1: Acute Delirium Management (Days 1–3)

  • Non-pharmacological delirium bundle: bright daytime light, consistent nursing staff, family presence, hearing aids and spectacles in situ, clock visible in room, gentle reorientation
  • Clonazepam tapered (not abruptly stopped — risk of withdrawal seizures): dose halved on Day 2, halved again on Day 4, target discontinuation over 2 weeks with GP handover
  • Hydroxyzine stopped immediately (short half-life, no taper required)
  • Chlorpheniramine stopped
  • Diclofenac replaced with paracetamol 1 g TDS and topical diclofenac gel (retaining some analgesia without systemic NSAID exposure)
  • Renal function, electrolytes, TFTs, blood glucose — checked. No metabolic precipitant found.

Phase 2: De-prescribing and Optimisation (Days 4–10)

  • Metformin continued (eGFR 58 — just above Beers threshold; monitored)
  • Insulin glargine dose reviewed — hypoglycaemia-induced delirium possible contributor; dose titrated down 2 units with dietitian input
  • START criteria assessed: bisphosphonate not prescribed despite FRAX fracture risk >20% → calcium, vitamin D, and alendronate initiated after renal function confirmed acceptable
  • Sleep hygiene counselling substituted for benzodiazepine for long-term insomnia

Outcome

By Day 7, delirium had fully resolved. MoCA improved to 22/30 at discharge (approaching pre-morbid baseline). Barthel Index rose to 75/100. The patient was discharged with five medications (down from nine), a written de-prescribing plan for the GP, and a family education session on fall prevention and medication safety.

Discussion

This case illustrates two compounding phenomena: the anticholinergic burden problem (three separate anticholinergic/sedating medications prescribed by different providers over different years) and the prescribing inertia problem (a benzodiazepine prescribed for insomnia four years ago that was never reviewed for a patient who had meanwhile turned 77, then 78…).

The Beers Criteria 2023 is not a list of drugs you can never give older adults. It is a list of drugs that require active justification, documented monitoring, and ideally a defined review date. The moment that documentation disappears, the drug becomes a liability.

De-prescribing is a clinical skill. It requires the same cognitive effort as prescribing. The default should not be “continue until proven otherwise” — it should be “justify continuation at each encounter.”
