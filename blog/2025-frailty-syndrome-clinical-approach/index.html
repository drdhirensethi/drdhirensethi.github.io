Frailty Syndrome: A Practical Clinical Approach Using Fried Criteria

A practitioner's guide to identifying and staging frailty in older adults using the Fried Frailty Phenotype, with clinical pearls from the geriatric ward at a tertiary military hospital.

Published 15 September 2025 8 min read
GeriatricsFrailtyClinical AssessmentAgingCGA

Frailty is not a diagnosis we find in a blood report. It is a clinical phenotype — a state of increased vulnerability to stressors — that every clinician caring for older adults must be able to recognise and stage at the bedside.

What Is Frailty?

The Fried Frailty Phenotype, published by Linda Fried and colleagues in 2001, remains the most widely validated frailty model in clinical practice. It defines frailty across five domains:

  1. Unintentional weight loss — more than 4.5 kg or 5% body weight in the past year
  2. Exhaustion — self-reported fatigue on two or more days per week (CES-D items 7 and 20)
  3. Weakness — grip strength below gender- and BMI-adjusted thresholds (hand dynamometer)
  4. Slow gait speed — 4-metre walk test below 0.8 m/s (or 1.0 m/s in some cutoffs)
  5. Low physical activity — energy expenditure below 383 kcal/week (men) or 270 kcal/week (women) on the Minnesota Leisure Time Activity questionnaire

Scoring:

  • 0 criteria → Robust
  • 1–2 criteria → Pre-frail
  • ≥3 criteria → Frail

Why Does It Matter?

Frailty is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes: falls, hospitalisation, disability, and mortality. In our geriatric ward at Command Hospital (SC), frailty stage at admission consistently predicts length of stay and discharge destination better than any single laboratory marker.

Clinical Pearls

1. Grip Strength Is Your Fastest Screen

A hand dynamometer is inexpensive, reproducible, and takes less than two minutes. Use gender- and BMI-stratified cutoffs (Fried 2001, Table 2). A single low reading in a 75-year-old should trigger the full assessment.

2. Gait Speed Predicts Everything

The 4-metre gait speed test is sometimes called the “sixth vital sign” in geriatrics. Pace < 0.8 m/s correlates with cognitive decline, falls risk, and 5-year mortality in community-dwelling older adults.

3. Pre-frailty Is the Window for Intervention

Frailty itself is partially reversible, but pre-frailty is the window where resistance training and nutritional supplementation show the strongest evidence for reversal. Document and flag pre-frail patients for physiotherapy and dietetics referral.

4. Context the Score With Comorbidity Burden

A Fried score of 3 in a 70-year-old with controlled hypertension tells a different story than the same score in an 85-year-old with advanced CKD, heart failure, and cognitive impairment. Always pair the frailty score with a Charlson Comorbidity Index.

Integrating Frailty Into Surgical Risk Assessment

One of the highest-impact uses of frailty assessment in a military hospital context is pre-operative risk stratification. Frail patients (score ≥3) have 2–3× higher rates of post-operative complications, delirium, and institutional discharge compared to robust patients matched for age and procedure type.

Before any elective surgery in a patient over 70, a frailty screen should be as routine as an ECG.

The Geriatric Anesthesia Assessment Sheet

Our department is piloting a structured Geriatric Anaesthesia Assessment Sheet that embeds Fried frailty scoring alongside the Barthel Index, MoCA screen, and polypharmacy review. The goal is to give anaesthesiologists and surgeons a single-page geriatric risk snapshot before they see a patient in the pre-op clinic.
